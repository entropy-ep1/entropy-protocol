 It is the fact that, with the yield on the U.S. 10-year Treasury note at around 1.35%, the risk of capital loss in the U.S. bond market is so high that it has become virtually impossible to maintain a pension fund’s funding level. At the same time, the sharp drop in the price of oil and other raw materials, which are often the main export products of EM countries, is further reducing EM growth and compounding the problem. The result is that pension funds and insurers are desperately looking for higher yields, often in untested EM securities.
This is particularly true in Japan, where pension funds are expected to invest a record 1.5 trillion yen ($13.4 billion) in emerging markets this year. These investors are now more than ever looking to diversify their portfolios away from Japan’s low interest rates and a weak yen, which is now at a 15-year low against the U.S. dollar. Japan’s Ministry of Finance recently estimated that Japanese pension funds will increase their allocation to foreign stocks and bonds to 20% by 2018 from 14.3% in 2010. According to the Japan Pension Service, 27% of the total assets of Japanese pension funds were invested in Japanese stocks and bonds as of the end of September 2014, compared with 41.2% in 2007.
The search for yield is also forcing insurers to look for alternatives. The long-term nature of their business, which has an average investment horizon of 15 years, has made it impossible for insurers to find suitable investment vehicles in Japan. Many are now looking to EM countries, which are more than willing to offer longer-term fixed-income securities.
